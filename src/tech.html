About the Tech

How we use Aptamer to mimic Red Blood Cells

Everything from how we chose the tech to it's development and role in the body.

What even is an aptamer?

Our DNA is made up of two chains (polynucleotide) that coil around each other. Each chain has basses attached to it, you can think of them like magnets. The basses like attaching to another “complimentary” base.

An aptamer is a single one of these chains that instead of attaching to a different chain, rolls itself up into a tangle, a bit like a pair of headphones getting tied up in your pocket. Depending on what basses are attached to the chain, the aptamer rolls up into a different shape and can hold on to different molecules.

Our goal with Aptamer Based Oxygen Transporters (ABOT) is to ensure that an aptamer can hold onto a heme molecule in a way that the heme is able to pick up and drop off oxygen.

Heme is the active molecule in the hemoglobin present in your red blood cells and is responsible for oxygen transport, but can also pick up other molecules (we’ll have to deal with that later).

How we evolve it

To make sure our aptamers are rolling up in the right way and holding onto the right things, we’re going to use the same theory that led to humans in the first place: evolution! Evolution is the change in the characteristics over several generations. Natural selection is the mechanism in evolution that keeps the best, and discards the rest.

We’re going to apply evolution to aptamers by letting them roll up, giving them opportunities to attach onto heme and oxygen, and then eliminate those who didn’t hold on properly. We then clone all the aptamers that did a good job while inducing mutations to progress faster and then tangle, attach, eliminate until we have aptamers that are performing as we want.

This process should take from a couple of days to a month. Once we have a good aptamer, we will sequence it’s DNA (pretty much taking notes on how it’s designed for later usage). This is what lets us make the evolution process a one-off thing.

How we modify the aptamer to be stable

With the aptamer’s DNA at our fingertips, we’ll transcribe it’s information into a different form called Locked Nucleic Acid (LNA). This is very similar to DNA but protects the information from being degraded by the body’s enzymes.

The last step after transcription is PEGylation which is just the addition of polyethylene glycol (PEG) to the LNA, protecting our aptamers from filtration in the kidneys.

These two steps mean that ABOT can stay in the blood for potentially hundreds of days, instead of 10-20 hours as seen in other past synthetic oxygen transporters.

Manufacturing

At this point in the process, we have a blueprint on how to produce ABOT. There are now many possible paths for manufacturing and scalability. The best case is a new form of PCR that allows us to very cheaply produce insane amounts of DNA and LNA bringing the price of ABOT to sub $100. There is reason to believe this is possible since some variations of the enzymes used in PCR are able to unreliably incorporate LNA.

After manufacturing, we will partner with organizations like hospitals and Blood Services Canada to distribute these blood substitutes to those in need.
